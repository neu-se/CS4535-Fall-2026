## Which Defense Would You Add?

<div style={{fontSize: '0.54em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.2em'}}>

**January 31, 2017, late at night.** A GitLab engineer is fixing a database replica that has fallen behind. The plan: wipe the replica's data and copy it fresh from the primary. They run the delete command in the wrong terminal. By the time they stop it, **300 GB of production data is gone**.

GitLab has **five** backup and replication systems.

</div>

<div style={{fontSize: '0.54em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.5em'}}>

**Which one missing defense would have made tonight boring?**

**A.** A guard that blocks deletes on the primary

**B.** More backup copies

**C.** An alert when a backup fails

**D.** A regular drill that restores from a backup

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**Run it exactly as the Alaska vote ran on Sep 17: simultaneous show of hands, fingers on four.** Read the scenario, say "pick one, don't put a hand up yet", fifteen seconds of quiet, then count down. Say the distribution out loud so it exists as a fact.

**Do not name the categories.** A is Prevent, B is more Recover, C is Detect, D is Test applied to Recover. They will get those names in ten minutes, and the vote is better if they choose on instinct now.

**Ask two people who voted differently to say why, one sentence each, and do not adjudicate.** The next slide does.

**Expect A and B to do well.** A because a guard is what engineers are trained to want. B because "back up more" feels like the obvious fix for losing data. B is the one the reveal takes apart.

**On the engineer:** GitLab's own postmortem is blameless, and so is this telling. Nobody is the villain. If somebody asks, the engineer was working late on a real problem, and ran the right command against the wrong server.

> **Transition:** Here's what they had.
</aside>

## Five Backups. None Worked.

<div style={{fontSize: '0.52em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.2em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.4em'}}>

**Nightly dumps** used the wrong Postgres version. The bucket was **empty**.

**The emails** reporting that failure were being silently rejected. Nobody saw them.

**Cloud disk snapshots** had never been turned on for the database.

**Replication** was already broken. That was what the engineer was fixing.

</div>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**What saved them:** a snapshot someone had taken **by hand, six hours earlier**, to refresh staging. Six hours of data lost; eighteen hours to copy it back.

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.6em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
B was never the answer. They had five. A defense you haven't tested is a defense you don't have.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Read the red box one line at a time, pausing after each.** The emails line is the one that gets a laugh: The Detect layer existed, and its alerts were being dropped because of an email-signing setting.

**In GitLab's words,** from the Feb 1 post: out of five backup and replication techniques, "none are working reliably or set up in the first place." From the postmortem, on the root cause: "there was no ownership ... nobody was responsible for testing this procedure." Quote that second one. It is the thesis of the session in their words.

**Be precise about the save, because students will repeat it.** The recovery did come from an LVM snapshot, which is one of the five mechanisms. It worked only because somebody had happened to take one by hand six hours earlier to refresh staging. The scheduled one was a day old. So the honest line is "the save was luck", not "no backup existed".

**Now adjudicate the vote.** B was never the answer: They had five. C would have told them the dumps were empty, if the alert itself had worked, so Detect needs testing too. D is the one that would have made the night boring: A single restore drill finds the empty bucket in the first minute. And A, the guard, is worth having, but ask the room: how would they have known it worked?

**Say the fragment and then leave it.** Do not name Prevent, Test, Detect, and Recover yet. Arc 1 does that, and it works better if they have just watched all four fail together.

**What was lost, if asked:** about six hours of database data, roughly 5,000 projects, 5,000 comments, and 700 new user accounts. Git repositories themselves were not affected.

## It Could Break. Now What?

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.6em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.62em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.6em'}}>

## Prevent First. Then Layers.

<div style={{fontSize: '0.5em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.14)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.5em'}}>

**Prevent.** Can you design it out? A constraint, a type, a foreign key that refuses the bad state. *Then ask whether the guard holds, because nothing is impossible until you've checked.*

</div>

<div style={{display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1fr 1fr', gap: '0.35em'}}>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Test.** It fails before it ships, and that includes the guard.

</div>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Detect.** You find out in production, before a user tells you.

</div>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(155,93,229,0.12)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Recover.** You can undo it. Backups, restore, a flag you can turn off.

</div>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(120,120,120,0.10)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Accept.** None of the above, on purpose, with the reason written down.

</div>

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.58em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
These stack. Blast radius decides how deep the stack goes. Likelihood is the question you reach for first and the least useful.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Reveal one box at a time and map each to what the room just said.** "Add a constraint" is Prevent. "Write a test" is Test. "We'd see it in Sentry" is Detect. "Roll it back" or "restore from backup" is Recover. Leave Accept for last and ask whether anybody said it. Usually nobody did.

**Prevent sits on its own row because it is a different kind of answer.** It is the one you reach for at design time, and when it works the failure cannot happen at all. But a guard is code too. The `RESTRICT` clause you meant to write and the `CHECK` constraint on the wrong column are both preventions nobody checked. So the question after Prevent is "and how do I know it holds?"

**The four below are layers, not alternatives.** GitLab had Recover five times over and Detect on top of it, and they all failed together, because none had been exercised. A high-blast-radius failure gets several layers, and each layer needs its own evidence that it works: a test of the guard, a restore drill for the backup, an alert that fires when the backup does not run.

**Recover is the one they have not heard named.** Yesterday's flags and ramps are Recover, because turning a flag off undoes the change. Backups, point-in-time restore, and a down migration are Recover for data. A failure is reversible only if somebody built a way to reverse it.

**Accept needs defending immediately or the room will hear "be lazy".** It is a decision with a written reason: the blast radius is small, the failure announces itself, a human can fix it by hand in a minute. "I didn't get to it" is a different sentence. Accept is also the only box that excludes the others: It means zero layers.

**Three minutes, no more.** The boxes are a reference for the activity, not a lecture.

> **Transition:** Five ways your column-groups feature could go wrong. For each one: can you prevent it, and what goes on top?

## Five Failures, Five Stacks

<div style={{fontSize: '0.42em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.2em'}}>

| | Blast radius | How you'd find out | Prevent? | **Then** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Wrong grouping, and nobody can tell | **An instructor, weeks later** | No. A constraint can't know the right group | **Test** · Recover |
| **B** | **Our biggest customer** can't use the gradebook | From them, loudly, in week 3 | No | **Test** · Detect |
| **C** | One group has the wrong name. No scores touched | Whoever looks at the header | Not worth it | **Accept** |
| **D** | **A semester of grades, gone** | Instantly, and too late | **Yes.** `ON DELETE RESTRICT` | **Test the guard** · Detect · **Recover** |
| **E** | The feature exists and nobody uses it | Only from watching usage | No | **Detect** · Recover |

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.56em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
C and D are both rare. C gets one sentence in the PR. D gets every layer there is. Likelihood didn't decide that; blast radius did.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Walk them in order, about a minute each, then spend what is left on the fragment.**

**A: no Prevent, then Test and Recover.** No constraint can know which group a column belongs in, so it cannot be designed out. A column in the wrong group looks exactly like a column in the right one, so nothing in production will flag it either. That leaves Test: run the old memo and the new backfill over every seeded course and assert they agree. Recover is cheap here, because the backfill can be re-run. Ask who wrote that comparison test, and who wrote it *before* the backfill.

**B: Test and Detect.** CS 2100 is our biggest and most important customer, and its shape is known: 500 students, 400 columns. A failure you can describe in advance is one you can test for, so seed that shape and assert the page loads within a budget. Detect goes on top because real courses will find shapes the seed doesn't have. If a pair wrote only Detect, ask who finds out first. It is the instructor we can least afford to disappoint.

**C: Accept, and nothing else.** It needs two instructors editing the same group's name at the same moment. That essentially never happens, and if it does, the group ends up with one of the two names they typed, somebody renames it, and no grades were touched. Preventing it means optimistic locking or a conflict prompt on a label: version columns, a retry path in the UI, and tests for both, to protect a string anyone can fix in five seconds. Accept here means one sentence in the PR: concurrent renames are last-write-wins.

**D: Prevent, and then everything on top of it.** The guard is one clause: `ON DELETE RESTRICT` or `SET NULL` on the column's group reference. Ask the room to open their own migration and check what they wrote. Some will find `CASCADE`. Give that finding time. It matters more than anything else today. Then the layers: a test that deleting a group cannot delete a score, because the guard is only a guard if it holds. Recover, because the guard will not cover the failure nobody thought of: Supabase's point-in-time restore, and a restore drill so you know it works. Detect: an alert when a backup does not run. This is GitLab, in miniature: The restore drill is the layer they did not have.

**E: Detect, then Recover.** Every assertion passes, because the editor does exactly what you built. What failed is the gap between that and what an instructor expected. You find it by watching usage: how many open the editor, how many save a change, where they stop. Recover is a flag: ship the editor behind one and you can pull it while you redesign.

**Then the fragment, and pause on it.** C and D are equally unlikely, and both start with an instructor editing a group. One gets zero layers; the other gets all of them. Likelihood cannot tell them apart.

## Your PR Is Green. So What?

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

A DOM assertion asks whether it's there. It cannot ask whether it looks right.

<aside className="notes">
**Ask the room how they would assert against each one before you reveal the fragment.** They will get partway on two of them and stall on the others, and the stalling shows why the next idea is needed. You can assert a computed color; you cannot reasonably assert that gray on gray is unreadable. You can assert a width; you cannot enumerate every way a layout can break.

**The third item is real and recent in this codebase**, which is why it is on the list. Wide tables that overflow their container are one of the standing accessibility defects, and their column-groups work touches exactly that area.

**Then say what the alternative is, in one line.** Rather than describing what correct looks like in assertions, you capture what it looked like when it was correct and compare. That is visual acceptance testing, and it is the only technique here that does not require you to predict the failure in advance.

**Say this argument twice.** Every other test in this session catches something you thought of. A screenshot diff catches things nobody thought of, which no assertion-based test can do.

**Then the cost, in two sentences.** Screenshots are fragile: A late-loading font or an avatar that arrives a frame late is a diff, so the suite has to wait for the page to settle before every capture. And a visual test fails when you are *different*, not when you are wrong, so every intentional redesign turns it red and somebody has to approve the diff.

**Point back at the screenshots in their own `gradebook-e2e` artifacts.** Collapsed and expanded, every run. That is this technique, applied to their feature, and it is how the grader looks at their grouping.

> **Transition:** So, which failures are worth a test at all?

## Test What Breaks Quietly

<div style={{fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.4em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Worth a test:** wrong numbers, half-applied migrations, permissions that leak, anything where the failure is invisible until it is expensive.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Usually not:** restating a label, asserting on the mock, or anything that fails only when you change it on purpose.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(120,120,120,0.10)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**And a test you will not maintain is worse than no test**, because it teaches the team to ignore red.

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.6em', marginTop: '0.55em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
A loud failure needs no test. It already tells you.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**The fragment compresses the whole session, so pause on it.** If a defect crashes the page on first load, you will find it in thirty seconds without a suite. The tests worth their cost are aimed at failures that look like success: a column in the wrong group, a role that can see one row too many, a backfill that handled four courses out of five. Row A, in other words.

**The gray box connects back to yesterday and should be said explicitly.** A test that fails at random, a skipped spec, and a test nobody updates all end in the same place, which is a team that has learned red does not mean broken. Every test you write is a commitment to keep maintaining it, and writing fewer, better ones is a legitimate engineering decision rather than laziness.

**Then connect it to Monday.** From Monday they own an area and they will be adding tests to a suite other people depend on. The question to ask is "what would have caught this quietly, and is that cheaper than finding out from a student in November".

**This slide is three minutes and it closes the session.** Tie it back to the debrief: A and B were the rows where Test was the right layer, and both break quietly. D's test is a test of the guard, and it is worth writing for the same reason.

**Read the gray box against row C.** A test you will not maintain, a guard for a case that does not matter, and an alert nobody acts on are the same mistake: work that looks like diligence and buys nothing.
