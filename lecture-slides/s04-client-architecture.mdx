**This is the claim of the session and everything else is a consequence of it.** Put it on the board.

**Next.js is here for routing, and very little else.** They will read that this is a Next.js app and reasonably assume a server tier, because that is what the framework's own documentation is mostly about. <aside className="notes">
**Monday's answer, extended by one half.** They know the pull side: a policy is a predicate Postgres staples onto every query, evaluated once per row, and there is no code path anybody can forget to call. That is why the browser is allowed to hold a Supabase client at all.

**The push side is the new part, and it is a genuinely nice piece of design.** Realtime changes do not arrive on one firehose that the client filters. They arrive on a topic, and the topic you are subscribed to is itself an authorization decision. Three of them matter: `class:<id>:staff`, `class:<id>:students`, and `class:<id>:user:<profile_id>`. A grade change that only staff should know about is broadcast on the staff topic, and a student's browser is not listening there.

**Say the general principle, because it is the transferable one:** RLS defends the data you asked for. The topic defends the data nobody asked for and that the server volunteered. Those are different problems and they need different mechanisms. A system that gets the first one right and pushes everything to everybody has still leaked.

**The box in the middle is the point of the diagram.** There is no authorization code in the component. Not "there shouldn't be" as a style preference; there is nowhere sensible to put it, because anything you write there is running on a machine the user controls. If a student's first instinct for "students shouldn't see unreleased grades" is to add a conditional in the render, this diagram is the answer, and Discussion 1 is about to test whether it landed.

**Cheap and worth it if somebody asks how the broadcast gets sent:** a trigger on the table, which is Monday's material again.

<aside className="notes">
**This is objective 2, and it is the only rehearsal they get before it counts.** Deliverable 3 next week and every ticket from Oct 15 onward is this judgment. Six minutes.

**Say "no code" and mean it.** Every one of the four is answerable by somebody who has never opened this repository, because the question is about responsibility rather than syntax.

**What each one is doing:**
1. **Rename a group.** Straightforward, and it is the warm-up: a write, so it needs a policy, and the schema needs somewhere to put the name. Most pairs get here. The follow-up if they are quick: who is allowed to, and where is that written down?
2. **Show a count.** Genuinely a component concern, and it is in the list so that the answer is not always "further down." Do not let them talk themselves out of it. If the rows are already on the client, counting them is free and belongs where it is displayed.
3. **Students never see an unreleased group.** This is the trap and it is the reason the slide exists. It reads like a conditional in a render, and a conditional in a render is not a security control, it is a suggestion. The right answer is an RLS policy, and the fragment is pointing here. If a pair says "hide it in the component," ask what happens when the student opens the network tab, and let them work out that the row already arrived.
4. **Class average per group.** The interesting one, and there is no single right answer. Computing it in the browser means shipping every student's score to every client, which is a privacy problem before it is a performance problem. An RPC computes it next to the data and returns one number. This is Monday's claim 1 again: the cost is what you touch, and here what you touch is also what you disclose.

**Report-out: take all six pairs, question 3 first.** Ask for the layer and the consequence, and put the tally for question 3 on the board. If most of the room said component, that is the most useful thing to happen in the session and you should say so rather than moving on politely.

**Watch for the failure mode** where a pair answers every question with "the database," having correctly learned Monday's lesson and over-applied it. Question 2 is the corrective and it is in the list for exactly that reason.

**Do not resolve question 4 fully.** Leave it open. It comes back in Arc 3 when they see what the client is already computing that it probably should not be.

<aside className="notes">
**This slide exists because half this room has already built this, in Java, for a grade.** If they took PDI2 they wrote `bind()`, they wrote a ViewModel, and they were told never to call `updateUI()` again. Name that in one sentence and let the people who recognize it feel it. Do not poll who took it, do not linger, and do not make anybody who did not take it feel late.

**For everyone else the left column is just a second example, not a prerequisite.** The sentence under both columns is the content. Read it out.

**Walk the right column in plain English and do not say the word "hook" until the fragment:**
- Line 1: remember a value, and start with whatever the cache already has, so the first paint is not empty.
- Line 2: tell the cache to call me when row `id` changes, and hand back the function that stops it calling me.
- Line 3: give the current value to whoever asked.

That is subscribe, update, unsubscribe. Anyone who has attached a listener to anything, in any language, has written this.

**The one real difference, and it is worth the fifteen seconds:** JavaFX has a scene graph that knows when a widget goes away, so `bind()` can clean up after itself. There is no scene graph here. The component can vanish at any time and nothing else knows, so the unsubscribe is written by hand, every time. That is the ugliest line on the slide and it is ugly for a reason.

**"A hook is a ViewModel" is the sentence to leave them with**, and for the PDI2 half it retires the whole mystery in four words. UI state, no UI dependency, testable on its own. The same thing L30 spent a session on.

**Do not go further into React than this slide goes.** No render cycle, no reconciliation, no dependency arrays. If somebody asks, the honest answer is that it matters and it is not what today is for, and the crash-course doc is linked from the last slide.

**The three bands are three granularities of the same idea**, and the third one is the one they have not seen before:
- Subscribe to the list, get told when anything changes. Coarse, correct, expensive.
- Subscribe to one row by id. This is `useGradebookColumn` from the last slide, and it is why a column header can repaint alone.
- Subscribe to a student and column pair. `useGradebookColumnStudent` does this, against a purpose-built index, and it is how one cell repaints while the other 15,999 sit still.

**L30's other answer was the extractor**, which you called more elegant and harder to understand, and you recommended against it for GA1. The bottom band is the extractor, built by hand, at production scale. If anybody took PDI2, that is a satisfying thing to be told.

**The transferable lesson, and say it in these words:** the granularity of your subscription decides the cost of a change. That is true in JavaFX, it is true here, and it will be true in whatever framework they are using in five years.

## One Query Per Table, Per Course

<div style={{fontSize: '0.5em', textAlign: 'left'}}>

```js
get gradebookColumns() {
  if (!this._gradebookColumns) {
    this._gradebookColumns = new TableController({
      client: this.client,
      table: "gradebook_columns",
      query: this.client.from("gradebook_columns")
                        .select("*")
                        .eq("class_id", this.courseId),
      classRealTimeController: this.classRealTimeController,
      initialData: this._initialData?.gradebookColumns
    });
  }
  return this._gradebookColumns;
}
```

</div>

<p style={{fontSize: '0.66em', marginTop: '0.5em', textAlign: 'left'}}>
Built on first use. One query, scoped to one course. Every component on the page shares it.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Twelve lines, and it is the whole idea of a controller.** Read it out loud line by line; there is nothing hidden in it.

**Four things to point at, in this order:**
1. **`if (!this._gradebookColumns)`.** Lazy. A page that never shows the gradebook never runs this query. There are roughly thirty of these getters on `CourseController` and most pages wake up three or four.
2. **`.eq("class_id", this.courseId)`.** The cache is per course, and so is the query. Ten courses share one database and this client holds rows for one of them.
3. **`classRealTimeController`.** This is where the arrows on the last slide come from. Handing it in at construction is what makes the cache self-updating rather than a snapshot.
4. **`initialData`.** The server prefetch from the first slide of this arc, landing here. If the layout already fetched the rows, the controller starts full instead of starting empty and flashing.

**The thing worth saying that is not on the slide:** the `.eq("class_id", ...)` is a convenience, not a defense. If a student edited that line to another course id, the query would still return nothing, because the policy runs regardless. Scoping is for cost; the policy is for safety. Monday's claim 1 and claim 2 side by side in one line of TypeScript.

**If asked why it is a getter and not a field:** so that constructing a `CourseController` is cheap, and so that a page pays only for the tables it actually reads. Thirty eager queries on every navigation would be a bad trade.

> **Transition:** One thing has to be true for any of that to typecheck.

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.68em', marginTop: '0.7em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>

<aside className="notes">
**Third time this has been said, and the first time it has been explained.** s02 listed it as a gotcha, Monday asserted it. Here it is a consequence, which is the only version that sticks: if the browser talks to Postgres, then the schema is the API, so the types have to be derived from the schema or they are fiction. There is no server in between with its own model classes to disagree with.

**Never hand-edit that file.** It is regenerated wholesale. An edit survives until the next person runs the command, which is a nasty failure because it is somebody else's build that breaks.

**The four commands are the loop they will be in all week**, and they are in the handout, but say the fourth one out loud because it is the one that wastes an afternoon: `db reset` drops the seeded class. Re-seed after every replay or you will be staring at an empty gradebook wondering what your migration broke.

**The fragment is the diagnostic worth memorizing.** The symptom of a missed `client-local` is type errors pointing at files you have never touched, because your new table is missing from the generated union and everything that reads the union goes red at once. A student who recognizes that symptom saves an hour; one who does not goes looking for a bug in `gradebookTable.tsx` that is not there.

**CI catches it, and that is worth thirty seconds** because it reframes what the check is for. `gradebook-e2e` replays every migration into a throwaway database and then compares the committed types against the schema that produced. Green does not mean the design is good. It means it applies, the types match, and something still groups. The design is assessed from the writeup.

## What the Memo Actually Does

<p style={{fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left', fontStyle: 'italic'}}>`gradebookTable.tsx`, around line 2556. React removed; this is all of it.</p>

<div style={{fontSize: '0.46em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

```js
for (const col of columns) {                      // sorted by sort_order
  const parts = col.slug.split("-");

  const base = (parts[0] === "assignment" && parts.length >= 3)
    ? parts[0] + "-" + parts[1]                   // assignment-lab, assignment-exam
    : parts[0] || "other";

  const contiguous = lastSortOrder === -1
    || col.sort_order === lastSortOrder + 1;      // no gap since the last one

  if (!contiguous || base !== currentGroupKey) {
    currentGroupKey = base;
    currentGroupIndex++;                          // start a new group here
  }

  groups[base + "-" + currentGroupIndex].columns.push(col);
  lastSortOrder = col.sort_order;
}
```

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.66em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
A group is a string prefix plus an unbroken run of integers. That is the entire concept.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Say "there is no React on this slide" before you say anything else.** Split a string, walk a list, compare to the previous one. Anyone who has written any language can read every line, and that is the point of showing it this way.

**Read it out and let the room reach the fragment before you do.** Two signals decide grouping: a prefix taken off the front of the slug, and a contiguity check on `sort_order`. Nothing else. There is no group table, no group id, no group anywhere in the schema. Monday they built one on paper. This is what it is replacing.

**The consequences fall straight out of the code and they should name them, not you.** Ask what happens if you delete a column in the middle of a group. The gap breaks contiguity, `currentGroupIndex` increments, and everything after it becomes a second group with the same display name. Ask what happens if you reorder. Group identity is positional, so the groups change. Both are in the handout and both are much better discovered here than read there.

**The one they will not get without help, and it is worth thirty seconds:** `assignment-lab-3` has three parts so it becomes group `assignment-lab`. `assignment-lab` has two parts, falls to the `else` branch, and becomes group `assignment`. The same family, two groups, decided by how many hyphens somebody typed.

**And the one that makes it permanent:** there is a BEFORE UPDATE trigger on `gradebook_columns` that refuses any change to `slug` once set. So the field that decides grouping is a field nobody is allowed to correct. Today the only way to move a column between groups is to delete it and lose its scores.

**Do not list all ten failure modes.** Three is enough to make the point and the rest are theirs to find; the Credit band is about finding them.

**The memo key is the smell and it is worth naming as a smell.** The real code hashes the entire column list with `JSON.stringify` and parses it back out inside the memo, because there was no stable identity to key on. When the cheapest way to say "has this changed" is to serialize everything, the thing you are grouping by is not a real identity. That is the argument for the assignment in one sentence.

> **Transition:** So replace it. Except the memo isn't the part that's hard.

## The Memo Is Not the Interface

<div style={{fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left'}}>

What sixty lines of slug-splitting produces:

</div>

<div style={{fontSize: '0.5em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

```ts
Record<string, { groupName: string; columns: Column[] }>
```

<div style={{fontSize: '0.62em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.8em'}}>

`groupedColumns` is referenced **18 times** in that file: the table headers, the virtualizer's height math, the collapse state, and the drag-to-reorder path.

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.78em', marginTop: '1em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
Keep the shape. Change where it comes from.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**This is the takeaway of the session. If they leave with one sentence, make it the fragment.**

**Say the trap out loud, because the handout's wording invites it.** Deliverable 3 says the memo stops splitting slugs, and it is easy to read that as "delete sixty lines, write a query, done." Then you start, and the table headers break, and the row heights are wrong, and dragging a column moves the wrong block, and it is Tuesday night. Eighteen call sites depend on the shape that memo returns, and none of them care where the rows came from.

**So the real shape of the task is small:** keep returning the same structure, and build it from rows you read instead of strings you split. What is left where the memo was should be doing nothing more interesting than shaping rows you were handed. That is the handout's own wording and this is what it means.

**Say this is deliberate guidance and not a spoiler.** Discovering the eighteen call sites the hard way costs a weekend and teaches nothing the objective asks for. The learning objective is recognizing that a derived value's *shape* is an interface with callers, which is a claim about design that holds in any codebase and in any language. They met the same idea in OOD under a different name.

**The good news to give them, and it is genuinely good news:** this is why a student who has never written React can do this assignment. You are not authoring components. You are changing where an existing function gets its input, and keeping its output the same.

**If somebody asks whether they can change the shape:** yes, at Distinction, and then they own all eighteen callers and should say so in the PR. That is a real claim and an assessable one. It is not the Pass path.

> **Transition:** One more thing before you go and do it, and it's the part people miss.

**Circulate and read over shoulders.** Six pairs, five minutes, you can see all of it. Decide from what you read which of the three debrief points to spend the next four minutes on.
