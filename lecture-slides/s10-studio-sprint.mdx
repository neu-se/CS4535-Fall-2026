<aside className="notes">
**Two minutes on the table, then one point that matters.** The rubric reads cadence: at least four substantive contributions a week, on at least three distinct days. A sprint worked in one Sunday is visible in the git history.

**Explain what a standup is, because most of them have never done one.** A short status update every team member gives on a schedule, named for meetings held standing up so they stay short. Three questions: what did you finish, what's blocking you and what do you need from whom, and what's next. The point is to surface a problem while a teammate can still help. A good post has links and a specific request under "Blocked"; "still working on it" isn't a post.

**Meeting summaries answer one question:** does anything we've learned change our plan?

<aside className="notes">
**Protect these ten minutes.** If Part 2 runs long, shorten the sprint-logistics slide, not this one. The sprint starts tomorrow and the instructor is away for two weeks, so nobody gets to ratify objectives or declare a band in person after today.

**Push on "what you'll be able to explain afterward".** That's what they're accountable for at meeting 2 and Checkpoint 1. An objective whose answer an AI could summarize in a paragraph isn't one. Reproducing a bug on its own no longer counts either; it has to be traced to where and why it fails.

**Ratify first, because the objective informs the band.** Someone aiming at Distinction's "evidence about users" should notice whether their sprint objective moves toward it.

**A sprint objective can come out of the study plan.** A team whose study runs early can make running it someone's objective (O7 and U8 on the menu), once recruiting is cleared with you. Say that, briefly, before they start.
