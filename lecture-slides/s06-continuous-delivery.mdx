## Staging Has Been Red Since Yesterday

<div style={{fontSize: '0.54em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.2em'}}>

Real, this week, in the repository you are about to merge into. `e2e-local` has failed on **every push** since PR #1016 landed. It isn't your commit.

</div>

```text
no-submission-grading-ui.test.tsx:100 › the staff grading route also
  redirects to Grade for a no-submission assignment

Expected pattern: /\/grade(?:\?.*)?$/
Received string:  ".../grade/assignments/103/submissions/72"
```

<div style={{fontSize: '0.54em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.5em'}}>

**A.** Revert #1016. It broke the build.

**B.** Fix the test. **C.** Fix the app. **D.** Not yours. Keep working on your branch.

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**Simultaneous show of hands, fingers on three, exactly as the Alaska vote ran on Sep 17.** Read it, fifteen seconds of quiet, then count down. Say the distribution out loud.

**The room cannot tell B from C from the slide, and that is the design.** Nothing here says whether the test or the app is right. A student who votes B or C confidently has decided something they have no evidence for, and that is worth naming gently when you take answers.

**What actually happened, and hold it until after the vote.** The test was stale and the app was correct. So C is wrong, B is right, and the only way to know is to read the branch history rather than the failure.

**Walk the timeline out loud. It is six beats and it is the best thing in this session, because the process failed in a way that looks like nobody making a mistake.**
- **Sep 17, 17:21.** The contributor adds the test, in `ff3fcc4f`. It passes. At that moment the staff route really does redirect to `/grade`, so the test is a correct description of the code.
- **Sep 21, 19:12.** I leave a review comment with a screenshot: submission history is throwing on no-submission assignments, please fix it.
- **Sep 21, 20:22.** They fix it, in `a242a30b`, by skipping the `/grade` redirect on the staff-prefixed route. This is the right fix. That route has no `grade` sub-page, so redirecting there would 404.
- **The test is now wrong, and nobody has touched it.** My comment changed the behavior. The test still describes the behavior from four days earlier.
- **Then the part I want you to sit with.** I ran the end-to-end suite locally on this branch and it passed. I ran it *before that last commit*. My evidence was real and it was true when I collected it, and by the time I approved, the thing I had verified no longer existed.
- **Sep 22, 00:19.** I approve and merge. Staging is red on every push from then until midnight last night.

**Now say the generalization, and it is the whole reason this slide is here.** Verification has a timestamp. Review is not a read-only operation: asking for a change *changes the thing you verified*, and the more useful your review comment, the more likely it invalidates your own testing. There is no amount of care that fixes this. The only defense is a machine that re-runs everything on every commit, which is the next slide's second bullet, and which in this case was skipped.

**Be fair to the contributor and say so plainly.** They wrote a test, responded correctly to review, and shipped the right behavior. I asked for the change and I did the final verification. If you want somebody to blame for a day of red staging, it is me, and it still would not have helped, because the failure is structural.

**Then the second failure, stacked on the first.** Even with stale human evidence, CI should have caught this on the commit that broke it. It did not run. All four of #1016's end-to-end runs skipped `e2e-local`, because the branch lives in a fork and the job is gated behind a trust check. **A skipped job satisfies a required check.** The merge button was green and the suite had not run.

**So two independent defences failed in the same direction, which is the honest shape of most incidents.** A human verified the wrong version, and the automation that exists precisely to catch that did not execute. Either one alone would have been survivable.

**Let that sit for a second before you move.** Everything in this session so far has been about red meaning something. This is green meaning nothing, which is strictly worse, because nobody investigates green.

**D is the option to come back to.** It is the same D as always: you have not avoided the problem, you have arranged not to see it. Every push after #1016 was red for everybody, so D scales to a team that never finds out.

**If asked why a fork skips it:** running someone else's code against your secrets on your runner is how supply-chain attacks work, so the caution is correct. The defect is that the skip counts as a pass. Say it in one sentence; the fix is a real design question and not this session.

**Point at the attribution, which was free here:** the last green run was the commit immediately before #1016. One change, one suspect, no bisect. That is the next slide, arriving early.

## Continuous Integration

<div style={{fontSize: '0.6em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

Everyone merges to one shared branch often, and **every change is built and tested automatically**, triggered by the commit and by the pull request.

</div>

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.6em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Never leave the build broken.** A red trunk blocks everybody, not just you. Fixing it outranks whatever you were doing.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Run on every change.** Not nightly, not before a release, and *actually run*: #1016's suite was skipped four times and the merge button stayed green.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**It does not replace testing before you push.** CI is the second opinion, not the first.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(120,120,120,0.10)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Feedback in minutes.** A suite that takes an hour gets ignored, worked around, and eventually turned off.

</div>

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**The four boxes are the ones that are actually contested, so spend the time on the two that sound wrong to a student.**

**"Never leave the build broken" sounds like a manners rule and is not, and they have just watched it fail.** On a shared trunk a red build stops every other person from knowing whether their own change is good, because they cannot tell your failure from theirs. Staging was red from Sep 22 until the small hours of this morning, which is roughly a day in which nobody merging could learn anything from CI. In five days they are all on that repository.

**The second box is where the vote lands, so read it against #1016 explicitly, and connect it to the timeline rather than only to the skip.** "Run on every change" is the rule that exists because human verification goes stale between commits. I tested that branch and then a commit landed after my test. "On every change" is what makes that recoverable, and a job that was skipped does not satisfy it, because the required check did not know the difference. The general form is worth one sentence: a gate that can pass by not running is not a gate, and it is the same failure as the triage verdict that could only come back "still real".

**"It does not replace testing before you push" is the one most likely to be ignored**, especially by anybody who has learned to use CI as a remote test runner. Pushing to find out costs ten minutes per attempt, and the loop that costs ten minutes is the loop you stop using.

**The last box is a real engineering constraint with a real threshold.** Somewhere around ten minutes people stop waiting for the result and start context-switching, and once they have context-switched the feedback has lost most of its value. Their own `gradebook-e2e` is not fast, which is worth admitting out loud rather than pretending the ideal is universal.

**If somebody asks who pays for all this compute:** somebody always does, it is usually invisible, and it is the reason the next slide's numbers are interesting.

## One Change Per Run

<div style={{fontSize: '0.62em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

If a run contains **one** change and it fails, you know who broke it.

If a run contains **fifty**, you have fifty suspects and a bisect ahead of you.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.6em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.7em'}}>

**Google's Test Automated Platform**, as of the numbers in the literature: about **50,000 changes a day**, roughly **4 billion test cases a day**, and an average wait of **11 minutes** for a result.

They do not run that because they enjoy it. They run it because a failure has to be attributable to a change, and the only way to keep it attributable at that rate is to test at that rate.

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.66em', marginTop: '0.6em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
Blame attribution isn't about who to be annoyed with. It's the difference between a ten-minute fix and a ten-hour hunt.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**This is the slide that explains why the rest of the session looks the way it does.** Trunk-based development, small batches, and pushing from master every few hours are all downstream of one idea: keep the number of suspects small. Say that here, because Arc 2 will otherwise sound like a preference about branching.

**The Google numbers do their work by being absurd.** Nobody in the room will ever build that, and that is fine. What they should take is the shape of the argument: at fifty thousand changes a day you can either test every change or give up on knowing what broke, and they chose to pay.

**The word "blame" will make somebody uncomfortable and the fragment is the answer.** This is not about finding who to be annoyed with. A failing run that points at one commit is a defect you fix before lunch; the same failure spread across a fifty-commit merge is a day of bisecting. Attribution is a property of the pipeline, not a judgment about a person.

**They have already seen this work, thirty seconds ago.** Finding the culprit for the red staging build took no bisect and no argument: the last green run was the commit immediately before #1016. One change per run bought that, and it cost nothing because the rate is low.

**Connect it to what they are about to do.** Thirteen people merging into one repository is a small enough rate that every pull request can be its own run, so they get attribution for free. They should notice they are getting it for free, because the moment a team stops getting it for free is the moment this stops being obvious.

## Passes Here. Fails In CI. Why?

```ts
await page.click('text=Add column group');
await page.waitForTimeout(500);
expect(await page.locator('.group-row').count()).toBe(3);
```

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.6em'}}>

Green every time on your laptop. Red about **one run in six** in CI, on the same commit.

**90 seconds, with the person next to you.** What's different about CI?

<aside className="notes">

**The answer they should reach: the test waits for the clock rather than for the condition.** Five hundred milliseconds is enough on an idle laptop and is not enough on a shared CI runner that is building three other things, so the assertion sometimes runs before the third row renders. Nothing about the code under test is wrong, and nothing about the test is wrong on the machine it was written on.

**The fix is one line and worth showing with your hands rather than a slide:** wait for the row count to reach three, with a timeout as the failure condition rather than as the strategy. `waitForTimeout` is the smell; waiting for the thing you are asserting on is the fix.

## When The Suite Lies

<div style={{fontSize: '0.62em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

A **flaky test** passes and fails on the same code. Same commit, same machine, different answer.

</div>

<div style={{display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1fr 1fr', gap: '0.8em', fontSize: '0.54em', marginTop: '0.6em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Where it comes from**

Waiting on something async and guessing how long. Two tests sharing state and running in a different order. Concurrency. A clock, a network, a random seed.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**What it costs**

Not the compute. The habit. A suite that cries wolf teaches the team to press rerun, and pressing rerun is how a genuine failure gets shipped.

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.62em', marginTop: '0.7em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
Flaky red says broken when nothing is. A skipped job says fine when nothing was checked. Both are the suite lying.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**The fragment now carries both halves, and #1016 supplied the second one for free.** Flakiness is a false red. A skipped required check is a false green. Students worry about the first and ship on the second, and the second is the one that cost this repository a day.

**Place this immediately after attribution, because it is the thing that dismantles it.** One change per run buys you one suspect, and a flaky test hands the suspicion to an innocent commit. A team that cannot trust red has paid for a pipeline and thrown away the result.

**Ask who has pressed rerun this week and expect most of the hands.** Then ask what they were doing when they pressed it: almost nobody re-reads the failure first. That reflex is the entire cost, and it is cheaper to notice it now, on an assignment, than in November on a shared trunk.

**The order-dependence case is worth thirty seconds because it is invisible.** Two tests that both write to the same seeded course pass in the order you happen to run them and fail when a runner shards them differently. Nothing changed in either test.

**Say where the evidence is and stop.** R1 on the reading list is Luo et al., FSE 2014, which took 201 commits that fixed a flaky test and sorted them by root cause. Async waits, test-order dependence and concurrency accounted for most of them. Somebody in this room may well be teaching that paper in October, so name it, say it is the empirical answer to "which of these actually happens", and hand the rest to them rather than spending it now.

**Do not go into detection or mitigation strategies.** Rerunning, quarantining and deterministic replay are a session of their own and not this one. What they need today is that red is a claim, not a fact.

## How Facebook Shipped Before 2016

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Your branch lived about a week.** You developed on it, alone, while master moved underneath you.

**A stabilize branch, weekly.** Everybody's week of work, merged together, then made to work as a set.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

## Trunk-Based Development

<div style={{display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1fr 1fr', gap: '0.8em', fontSize: '0.54em', marginTop: '0.4em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Long-lived branches**

Integration is an event. It happens late, all at once, and it is somebody's job.

Divergence grows all week. Conflicts are found at merge time.

The branch is how unfinished work is hidden.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Trunk-based**

Integration is continuous. Branches live hours or a day or two, then merge.

Divergence stays small. Conflicts are found while they are still small.

**A flag is how unfinished work is hidden.**

</div>

</div>

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.7em'}}>

Trunk-based costs you something real: your half-finished feature is now on master, running, in front of nobody.

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.64em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
Which only works if shipping the code and showing the feature are two separate acts.
</p>

**The third row is the hinge of the whole session and the fragment makes it explicit.** Both models have to hide unfinished work somewhere. The old one hides it on a branch, which is why integration hurts. The new one hides it behind a flag, which is why the next arc exists. If the room takes one thing from Arc 2, it is that trunk-based development is not available to you until you can turn a feature off.

**Be honest that trunk-based is not free.** Your unfinished code is running in production. It is being compiled, loaded, and in some cases executed on every request. That is a real cost and it buys you small merges.

**Tie it to the six days in front of them.** From Monday they are merging into a repository that ten courses depend on, in the middle of a semester, with short-lived branches and course-scoped flags. This slide is that arrangement, and it is the same arrangement for the same reasons.

An engineering event.

## CI, Continuous Delivery, Continuous Deployment

<div style={{fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Continuous integration.** Everyone merges to one shared branch often, and every merge is built and tested automatically. That's the CI already running on your pull requests.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.35em'}}>

**Continuous delivery.** Every commit that passes is *releasable*. Shipping is a decision somebody makes, and a button somebody presses.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Continuous deployment.** Every commit that passes *ships by itself*. No button, no decision, no human in the path.

</div>

</div>

<div style={{display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1.15fr 1fr', gap: '0.8em', fontSize: '0.56em', marginTop: '0.6em'}}>

<div>

Pawtograder does **continuous delivery**. Changes are promoted to production deliberately, not automatically. The button is a person.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(120,120,120,0.10)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.92em'}}>

**Why Facebook can't have a button.** By 2016 they took **1,000+ diffs a day**, so a weekly push carried up to **10,000**. When that breaks, you have 10,000 suspects. They moved to pushing from master every few hours instead.

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.62em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
<strong>Shift left:</strong> same defect, four prices. Your editor costs seconds, CI costs minutes, staging costs an hour, a student at 11pm costs an afternoon and their trust.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Two of these three sound the same and one letter apart is a genuinely bad naming decision that the industry is stuck with.** Say that out loud, because a student who thinks they misheard will stop listening. Delivery means it *could* ship. Deployment means it *did*.

**The distinction comes down to who decides.** Continuous deployment means no human between a passing test and a real user. That is a statement of enormous confidence in your tests, and it is the right answer for some products and a terrible one for a system holding grades.

**Where Pawtograder sits, and why, in one sentence:** twelve students merging into a platform that ten courses depend on mid-semester is not a place for nobody to be in the path. So a person decides when changes go to production, rather than every passing merge going out by itself.

**Shift left is the fragment, and it is the callback to the lifecycle diagram in Arc 1.** That slide named the direction and left it there. This one puts their own costs on it, which is why Arc 1 deliberately did not spend the prices. A type error caught by your editor costs seconds. Caught by CI, a couple of minutes. Caught on staging, an hour. Caught by a student at 11pm the night an assignment is due, it costs an afternoon, a Discord thread, and some amount of their belief that the tool works. Same defect, four prices. Every practice in this part of the course is an attempt to move the catch leftward.

**Arc 2 already told the pre-2016 half, so this sidebar is now a payoff rather than a story, and thirty seconds is enough. It is also the first thing to compress if the clock is tight.** It answers the question this slide provokes, which is "why would anyone want nobody in the path?" Chuck Rossi was their first release engineer and spent years running exactly the button you are running. By 2016 more than a thousand diffs a day were landing on master, and the weekly push sometimes carried ten thousand of them. The argument has nothing to do with humans being slow. A release containing ten thousand changes has ten thousand suspects when it breaks, and no human coordinating it can tell you which. Pushing fifty at a time gives you fifty suspects. So continuous deployment is a strategy for making failures *attributable*, and it buys that by giving up the gate.

**Then the honest contrast, which is the useful part for them:** Pawtograder takes a handful of changes a week from twelve students, so the gate costs almost nothing and buys a lot. The right answer depends on your rate, not on your sophistication. A student who leaves thinking continuous deployment is simply the more advanced choice has learned the wrong thing.

## Dark Launch, Then Ramp

<div style={{fontSize: '0.62em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.4em'}}>

**Dark launch.** Deploy it with the flag **off**. The code is live, nobody can reach it, and you find out whether it breaks the build and the tests before it can break a user.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.4em'}}>

**Ramp.** Turn it on for a growing fraction, on a schedule you decide: yourself, then staff, then one course, then more. `1% · 5% · 25% · 100%`

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**The cost.** Both paths now exist in production, so both have to work. Your test matrix doubled the moment you added the flag.

</div>

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**These are the three terms the working block needs. Say each one out loud and write "ramp" on the board.** Twenty minutes from now they will read a card that says `1% Mon · 5% Tue · 25% Wed 2:00pm` and it has to mean something without explanation.

**The ramp is not a rollout schedule you set and forget.** It is a sequence of decisions, each one made with more evidence than the last. That framing is what the incident cards are about.

**Read the ramp ladder, do not dwell on it.** The numbers matter far less than the shape, and the two slides after this one are where the session's observability content now lives. If you are running behind at this point, this is the slide to take it from.

**Dogfooding came up two slides ago and this is where it becomes a tactic rather than a virtue.** Putting yourself at the front of the ramp is the cheapest exposure available, because you are the one user who will definitely notice and definitely not file a ticket about it in March.

**The third box is the honest cost and it is the cut candidate if you are behind.** A flag means every test that mattered now matters twice, once with the flag on and once with it off. Teams forget the off path, ship, ramp to 100%, and discover six weeks later that turning the flag back off no longer works, which means the rollback they were relying on is gone. That is worth saying in one sentence even in the compressed version.

**Name what a dark launch cannot cover, because PR #1021 is the live counterexample and it takes twenty seconds.** A flag hides application code. It does nothing for a change to the database, the cluster, or the schema. #1021 raises Postgres's `/dev/shm` from 64Mi to 1Gi, and there is no fraction of users you can apply that to: it restarts the primary, so it waits for a maintenance window. When students hit that distinction in their own projects, it is usually a migration, and Sep 17's four-step migration is the technique.

**Do not go into how the flag is evaluated or cached.** It is a clinic question, and the honest answer takes longer than the ramp material it would displace.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1% | 2 | 20 | 0.10 | |
| 10% | 19 | 200 | 0.10 | count went up 10x, **this is fine** |

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.7em', marginTop: '0.7em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>

</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Protect this slide. It is the one thing that makes the working block answerable rather than a guess.** If Arc 1 has to be compressed, cut the test-matrix box on the previous slide, not this. Everything here is three columns of division and it takes ninety seconds.

**Walk the middle row first, because it is the counter-intuitive one.** Nineteen errors instead of two looks alarming on a dashboard and means nothing has changed. Ten times the users, ten times the errors, same product. A team that pages itself on raw counts during a ramp will page itself every single time.

**Then the bottom row.** 0.24 against a baseline of 0.10. Something about the fiftieth percentile of users differs from the tenth, or the feature degrades under load, or a code path only some users reach is broken. You do not know which yet, and you do not have to in order to act.

**Say what the baseline is for, because it is easy to miss.** The 1% step exists so that the 10% and 50% steps have something to be compared against. A ramp that starts at 50% has no baseline and you cannot tell a regression from the weather.

**The numbers here are deliberately not the numbers on the cards.** Do not let anybody pattern-match the table twenty minutes from now. They have to do their own division.

**If a student asks what counts as "exposed":** users for whom the flag evaluated true and who reached the code path. Counting everybody in an enrolled course inflates the denominator and hides a real regression. That is a good question and a one-sentence answer.

**There is a live production example of the uneven denominator if you want it, and it is thirty seconds.** PR #1021, open right now, documents 190 user-facing 503s in 24 hours on Khoury prod. They are nowhere near evenly spread: `GET /submissions` took 117 of them. A per-route rate finds that immediately and a global error count buries it under everything that was fine.

**Foreshadow Oct 1 in one sentence, and do it here rather than at the end.** Every number in that table came from somewhere: an error tracker counting events, and something counting exposed users. That is telemetry, and where it comes from, what logs and metrics and traces each buy you, and how you run an incident are the Operations session on Oct 1. Today you only have to read the numbers. Nobody should leave thinking a dashboard is a thing that exists by default.

<Slide>

## Two Ways To Buy Confidence

<div style={{display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1fr 1fr', gap: '0.8em', fontSize: '0.52em', marginTop: '0.4em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Test-driven development**

Write and maintain a test per feature.

Bugs are located at the unit level, by a test that names them.

You find out **before** it ships.

Pays in: tests written, and tests kept working.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.55em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Continuous delivery**

Write and maintain high-level metrics.

Bugs are located by a rate that moved, on a feature you shipped alone.

You find out **after** it ships, from a few users.

Pays in: telemetry built, and telemetry kept honest.

</div>

</div>

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.7em'}}>

Ship one feature at a time and you get the same thing a unit test gives you: **one suspect.**

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.64em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
This is a budget rather than a choice. Every team spends on both, and most spend badly by never deciding.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Both columns are answers to one question: how do you find out your change is bad?** Put it that way first, because a room that has spent fifty minutes on flags and ramps can leave thinking continuous delivery replaced testing, and it did not.

**The structural similarity is the insight and it is on the slide.** A unit test isolates one behavior so a failure points at one thing. A ramp isolates one feature so a rate points at one thing. Same move, different stage, and once they see that, deploying one feature at a time stops being a rule and starts being obvious.

**The honest version of the trade, which Module 14 makes and is worth repeating:** teams doing heavy continuous delivery tend to write fewer integration and system tests, because a canary tells them faster and cheaper what a slow system test was going to tell them eventually. They do not write fewer unit tests. The thing that gets displaced is the expensive, slow, brittle end of the pyramid.

**The fragment is the takeaway and it should be said plainly.** Both cost real engineering time, forever. A team with excellent tests and no telemetry finds out from a student on Discord. A team with excellent telemetry and no tests spends its life diagnosing in production. The failure mode is not picking wrong, it is never having noticed there was a budget.

**Then hand off, in one sentence, and do not elaborate.** Tomorrow is the other half: what end-to-end, visual acceptance and performance tests can and cannot catch, and what they cost to keep working. Today was the half that watches production. Stop there.
