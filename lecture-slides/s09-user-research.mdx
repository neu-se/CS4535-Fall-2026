## Your Riskiest Guess

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.6em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.62em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.6em'}}>

**One person per team, 30 seconds.**

Read out your starred assumption. Is it evidence or a guess? If it's wrong, what happens to your plan?

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**Four teams, two minutes, then a minute of reaction. Keep it to four.** Write the four guesses where the room can see them.

**Listen for the three ideas that come next, and use their words to introduce each one.**
- A guess backed by "we asked people and they said they'd love it" is the first idea: what people say versus what they do.
- A guess backed by "we're students, we know" is the second idea: they are one kind of user.
- A team that says "we'd need a big survey to know" sets up the third idea: five people is enough to start.

**Don't correct a guess yet.** The point of reading them out is that everyone hears four plans resting on things nobody has checked.

> **Transition:** Here's the first trap in how you'd check them.
</aside>

## What They Say, What They Do

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.4em'}}>

**"Would you use a live queue board?"** Everyone says yes. It costs nothing to say yes.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**"The last time you went to office hours, how did you decide when to go?"** Now you hear what they actually did, including the parts they'd never think to mention.

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.6em', marginTop: '0.6em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
Ask about a specific time in the past, or watch them do the task. Opinions about the future are free.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Open with a team's own example if you heard one.** If a team wrote "students want X" as evidence because somebody said so, use that.

**The mechanism in one sentence:** a question about the future asks people to predict themselves, and people are bad at it and polite about it. A question about a specific past time asks them to remember, which is much more reliable, and it surfaces the workarounds they have stopped noticing.

**Name the source if anybody wants more:** Rob Fitzpatrick's *The Mom Test* is a short book built entirely on this rule.

**Two minutes, no more.**

> **Transition:** The second trap is thinking you already know.
</aside>

</Slide>

## Five Is Enough to Start

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.4em'}}>

**Five people, in the right role, doing a real task.** Most of the problems that stop people show up within the first few sessions.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.4em'}}>

**Ask them to think out loud.** Then keep quiet and don't help. The moment you help is the moment you stop learning.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(120,120,120,0.10)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**You're finding problems, not measuring them.** Five people can't tell you what percentage of TAs will use a feature. They can tell you where it breaks.

</div>

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**The source, if asked:** Jakob Nielsen's 2000 article "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users". The argument is about usability problems in one design, and it says to run several small rounds rather than one big one. It is not a claim about surveys or statistics, and the third box is there so nobody leaves thinking it is.

**The "don't help" rule is the one they'll break.** Every developer who watches someone struggle with their UI wants to point. Tell them it's normal, and that the struggle is the data.

**Two minutes, then straight into Part 2.**

> **Transition:** You don't need working code to run one.
</aside>

</Slide>

## Study It Before You Build It

<div style={{fontSize: '0.56em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

<div style={{display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1fr 1fr', gap: '0.4em'}}>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Paper or whiteboard sketch.** Minutes to make. Good for "do they understand what this screen is for?"

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Clickable mockup.** An hour or two. Good for "can they find it, and do they click where you expect?"

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**A flagged build on staging.** Real data, real flow, hidden from everyone else. Good for "does it hold up in a real task?"

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(155,93,229,0.12)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**A person behind the curtain.** You do by hand what the software would do. Good for "is this worth automating at all?"

</div>

</div>

</div>

<p className="fragment" style={{fontSize: '0.58em', marginTop: '0.5em', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#e76f51'}}>
Use the cheapest prototype that can still prove your starred guess wrong.
</p>

<aside className="notes">
**Two minutes, and tie each box to a team if you can.**
- **Sketch:** Office Hours could sketch a queue status view on paper and ask a student what it tells them.
- **Mockup:** Usability could mock a permissions screen and watch an instructor try to give a TA grading rights without solution-repo access.
- **Flagged build:** every team already works behind course feature flags on staging, so this is nearly free once something exists.
- **Person behind the curtain:** Paper Exams could hand-grade a few scans and present the result as if an LLM had suggested it, to see whether TAs trust a suggestion before anybody builds the provider layer. The name for this is a Wizard of Oz study.

**The fragment is the rule.** A prototype only has to be real enough to test the one guess. Anything more is building the feature before you know whether it's the right one.

> **Transition:** Which leads to when your team should run its study.
</aside>

</Slide>

## Everyone Runs a Study. When Is Up to You.

<div style={{fontSize: '0.54em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.2em'}}>

**New Pass requirement:** a study of your project area with at least five real users, written up in your team repo by Checkpoint 3 (Dec 3).

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(255,180,0,0.15)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px', margin: '0.4em 0 0.3em'}}>

**Early, as a sprint objective (Oct 2–13).** The study decides what you build. *Likely: Office Hours, Usability.*

</div>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(100,150,255,0.12)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px', marginBottom: '0.3em'}}>

**Mid-project, on a prototype (Oct 14 – Nov 5).** You have a design and no code yet.

</div>

<div className="fragment" style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.45em', borderRadius: '8px'}}>

**Late, on what you shipped (before Nov 18).** The study checks whether it worked. *Likely: Paper Exams, Cloud Workspaces.*

</div>

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**Say the requirement first and plainly, because it's new.** It's now in the Pass list in the syllabus, under LO6. Every student needs to have helped plan, run, or analyze it, which a team of three or four can share.

**Why it's Pass and not optional:** LO6 is "decide from evidence about real users rather than assertion", and before today the only Pass item for it was shipping a user-visible change. Shipping without ever watching a user is exactly the assertion LO6 is about.

**The three timings follow from what the study is for.**
- **Early:** the study decides what to build. Office Hours and Usability both pitched a study before building, and O7 and U8 on the spike menu are protocols for exactly that. A team can run the first sessions inside the sprint if recruiting is approved in time.
- **Mid:** a prototype test before committing to a design. Any team can do this.
- **Late:** the study checks whether the change worked. Paper Exams and Cloud Workspaces are building infrastructure first, so their natural study is a real instructor grading real scans, or a real intro student opening a workspace, after the thing exists. Remind them that a late study needs a baseline, which means running the same tasks on today's system now.

**Late is not "later, maybe".** Feature freeze is Nov 18, and a study that runs after that can't change anything.

**Recruiting goes through you.** Say so, and say why: these are TAs and students in other courses.

> **Transition:** Now plan yours.

## Part 2: A Study That Could Prove You Wrong

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(42,157,143,0.12)', padding: '0.6em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left'}}>

**In `user-research.md`, about 17 minutes.** Plan a study of your starred guess:

- What you want to learn, in one sentence
- **When:** early, mid-project on a prototype, or late on what you shipped
- **Who:** five people in the right role, and how you'd reach them
- **Three or four tasks, or "the last time you..." questions**
- What you'd watch for
- **What result would change your plan**

Don't contact anyone yet. Bring this plan to tomorrow's session.

</div>

<div style={{backgroundColor: 'rgba(231,111,81,0.15)', padding: '0.5em', borderRadius: '8px', fontSize: '0.55em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.5em'}}>

**Last 5 minutes: swap.** Office Hours with Usability, Paper Exams with Cloud Workspaces. Find one leading question and one assumption their study doesn't test.

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**Put a visible timer up: 17 minutes, then 5 for the swap.**

**Circulate and read the question list.** The most common problems, in order:
- **Questions about the future.** "Would you use...", "How likely are you to..." Send them back to the first idea.
- **Questions that describe the solution.** "Would a status board help you decide when to go?" has already told them the answer.
- **No stopping rule.** A plan without a "what would change our plan" line will confirm whatever the team already believes. Ask them to write down the result that would make them change course before they collect anything.

**Paper Exams and Cloud Workspaces may say their users are unreachable.** They aren't: you teach courses with instructors and TAs, and every student in this room has been an intro student. Help them name five real people.

**For the swap, the reviewing team writes in the other team's file.** That leaves a record, and it forces a pull and a merge, which is fine practice.

**Don't make them contact anyone.** Recruiting TAs and students from other courses goes through you, and the plans get ratified tomorrow along with sprint objectives.

> **Transition:** One last thing: what to do with what you find.
</aside>

## From a Finding to a Ticket

<div style={{fontSize: '0.58em', textAlign: 'left', marginTop: '0.3em'}}>

| | |
|---|---|
| **What we saw** | One specific observation, not an opinion |
| **Who, and how many** | "4 of 5 TAs" |
| **What it cost them** | Time, errors, a workaround, giving up |
| **The change we suggest** | |
| **How we'd know it worked** | The same task, run again after the change |

</div>

<aside className="notes">
**This is also at the bottom of the handout, so keep it to two minutes.**

**The point of the format is the first two rows.** A ticket that says "users find the queue confusing" can't be acted on or checked. A ticket that says "4 of 5 TAs scrolled past the oldest request because the list sorts by name" can be fixed, and the fix can be tested by running the same task again.

**Connect it to s08.** A claimable ticket needed a way to know it's done. For a research finding, that's the last row.

> **Transition:** Before you go: your sprint objective.
</aside>
